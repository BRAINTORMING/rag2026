Simple Iron Ion Enables Photocatalytic Hydrogen
Evolution from Methanol
Masaya Sakurai
1
, Yudai Kawasaki1
, Takahiro Matsumoto
1
1. Kyushu University
Abstract
We report the discovery of a remarkably simple homogeneous catalyst – iron (Fe) ion
–that conducts hydrogen gas (H2) evolution from methanol under ultraviolet (UV) light
irradiation. Unlike previously reported homogeneous organometallic catalysts for methanol
dehydrogenation, our system functions without synthetic ligands and is conceptually distinct
from general heterogeneous and photoredox catalysts. The Fe ion catalysis achieved a turnover
frequency (TOF) of 149 h–1 and a production rate of 9.21 × 102 mmol gcat–1 h–1 for methanol
dehydrogenation with input of UV light (250–385 nm, 30 mW). These values place the Fe
ion catalyst within the upper performance range of previously reported systems – including
homogeneous, heterogeneous, and photoredox catalysts comprising both precious and nonprecious metals. The Fe catalytic system is not limited to lower alcohols; it can also be
applied to biomass and its derivatives. This simple and effective catalytic system, discovered
serendipitously, has no documented analogs, to the best of our knowledge.

hydrogen production, methanol, iron, photo

H2 production from alcohols represents a pivotal reaction for achieving carbon-neutral 
and sustainable societies, as both H2 and alcohols exhibit strong synergy with renewable 
energy and resources (1–7). Furthermore, the interrelationship between H2 and alcohols 
is particularly important in hydrogen carrier technologies. Notably, methanol, with a 
hydrogen content of 12.5 wt%, is widely regarded as a promising liquid hydrogen carrier. 
Against this backdrop, the dehydrogenation of methanol as well as other alcohols has 
attracted considerable attention. Recent research has focused particularly on the methanol 
dehydrogenation using homogeneous catalysts (8–25), heterogeneous catalysts (26–28), 
and photocatalysts (29–34). The homogeneous catalysis employed synthetic 
organometallic complexes based on noble metals (rhodium, iridium, and ruthenium) (8–
21) and non-noble metals (Fe and manganese (Mn)) (22–25). As substantially different 
from previous organometallic reactions, the reaction we discovered allows H2 to be 
generated from alcohols using an exceptionally simple catalyst, Fe ion, by input of light 
energy (Scheme 1). We stumbled upon this reaction not by design, but through a stroke 
of serendipity. This reaction was found during a control experiment as part of our 
investigation into H₂ evolution from methanol using organometallic Fe complexes. The 
input of light energy should be required to drive the thermodynamically endothermic 
nature of alcohol dehydrogenation. For instance, as the redox potential of methanol 
(HCHO + 2H+ + 2e–
 ⇄ CH3OH) is 0.232 V versus normal hydrogen electrode (35), the 
conversion of methanol to formaldehyde must need external energy.

We examined the ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectrum of iron(III) chloride 
(FeIIICl₃) in methanol, as the dehydrogenation reaction necessitated UV light irradiation.

NaOH is needed to accelerate the dehydrogenation from methanol. These absorption

(FeIII–O(H)CH₃ species). The LMCT can be used for the excitation of methanol- or 
methoxide-coordinated FeIII species. This process should induce electron transfer from 
methanol or methoxide to the FeIII center, likely resulting in the formation of an FeII 
species in which a methanol radical or methoxide radical might be weakly interacting 
with the FeII center. This LMCT is considered to be involved in the photocatalytic H₂ 
evolution from methanol, as UV light is required for this reaction. However, the H2 
production mechanism remains to be elucidated due to the lack of prior studies. 
We indeed examined H2 evolution from methanol catalyzed by FeIIICl3 under UV 
light irradiation. FeIIICl3 was dissolved in methanol and the resulting solution was 
exposed to UV light (250–385 nm, 10–30 mW) in a closed quartz cell capped with a 
septum. The gas phase was analyzed by gas chromatography (GC) after the photoreaction. 
As a result, H2 was detected from this remarkably simple system (Fig. S2). Surprisingly, 
in this system, the H2 evolution occurred even under air, in which the catalytic activity is 
comparable to that under an N2 atmosphere. While a small amount of H2 evolved without 
NaOH, the amount of H2 evolved was significantly increased on addition of NaOH into 
the system. As the concentration of NaOH increased, the turnover number (TON, mol of 
H2/mol of FeIII ion) of H2 evolution increased. To investigate the effect of NaOH on the 
photocatalytic reaction, we examined the H2 production reaction under varying 
concentrations of NaOH and plotted the resulting TON values (Fig. 1A). This plot reveals 
a saturation curve, analyzed using the steady-state approximation as in the MichaelisMenten kinetics model. Furthermore, the double reciprocal plot displays a linear 
correlation (Fig. 1B). This type of kinetics indicates that a pre-equilibrium is involved in 
the reaction. In this case, there should be the pre-equilibrium between the deprotonation 
of methanol and protonation of methoxide coordinated to the Fe center (Scheme 2). From 
the intercept and slope of the double reciprocal plot, the equilibrium (K) and rate constants 
(k) were determined as K = 21 and k = 6.9 × 10–3
 s–1
, respectively.

Investigation of Photocatalytic Reaction Conditions 
In the case of addition of acids such as sulfuric acid (H2SO4) and acetic acid 
(CH3COOH), there is no significant effect on H2 evolution reaction (Fig. 2). We also 
investigated the effect of changing the counteranions, NO3
–
 and SO4
2–
, in FeIII salts, and 
no significant influence on the H2 evolution reaction was observed (Fig. 3). Then, in order 
to optimize the conditions for the H2 evolution reaction, we varied the concentration of 
FeIIICl3, the light intensity, and the reaction time (Fig. 4). The reaction tends to proceed 
more facilely when the concentration of FeIII ion is reduced (Fig. 4A). As is commonly 
observed in photochemical reactions, increasing the concentration of photocatalysts often 
leads to a decrease in reactivity. The TON increases with higher UV light intensity (Fig. 
4B). From the time-dependent TON data, it was observed that the TON increases linearly 
up to 10–15 h, after which the slope tends to decrease (Fig. 4C). This is likely due to the 
increase in internal pressure caused by the H2 generated under the closed system. Control 
experiments without FeIII ion or UV light (250–385 nm) did not result in the formation of

Through the examination of various reaction conditions described above, we 
achieved the highest TOF (TON/hour) value of 149(SD = 3.62) h–1
 (TON of 448(SD 
=10.9) for 3 h) in our system at present, which was obtained from the photo-induced 
methanol dehydrogenation reaction using FeIIICl3 (0.017 mM) in methanol with NaOH 
(250 mM) under UV light irradiation (250–385 nm, 30 mW) for 3 h. This TOF value is 
almost comparable to those reported for H2 production from methanol using non-noble 
metal homogeneous catalysts. For examples, a homogeneous Fe complex, 
[Fe(H)(HBH3)(CO){HN(CH2CH2Pi
Pr2)2}], catalyzed the methanol dehydrogenation in 
methanol/water (9/1) including 8.0 M potassium hydroxide at 91 °C with a TOF of 644 
h–1
 (TON of 9834 for 46 h) (22). Another homogeneous Fe complex, 
[(
iPrPNHP)Fe(CO)(H)(HCOO)], catalyzed methanol dehydrogenation from a mixture of 
methanol/water (4/1, mol/mol) in ethyl acetate as a solvent including lithium 
tetrafluoroborate under reflux with a TOF of 543 h–1
 (TON of 51,000 for 94 h) (23). A 
Mn complex with excess HN(CH2CH2PiPr2)2 ligand can function as a stable 
dehydrogenation catalyst in methanol/water (9/1) including 8.0 M potassium hydroxide, 
reaching a TON of more than 20,000 over one month at 92 °C (TOF of ca. 28 h–1
 at 90 °C) 
(24). Additionally, an Fe complex with a o-aminophenolato ligand also functions as a 
dehydrogenation catalyst under light irradiation (25). Our system is fundamentally 
distinct from these organometallic systems, representing the first example of H2 
generation from alcohol using only a metal ion to the best of our knowledge. Despite the 
simplest catalytic system in the homogenous systems, the TOF achieved is on the same 
order of magnitude as those reported in previous homogeneous system. However, as 
discussed below, in our system, the H2 evolution from methanol under conditions where 
water is present exhibits a TOF that is two orders of magnitude lower than under 
conditions without water. These results indicate the mechanism for Fe catalysis should be

Application of the Fe Ion System to Other Lower Alcohols, Cellulose, and Related 
Compounds 
Our system can be applied to other lower alcohols, such as ethanol, 1-propanol, and 
2-propanol. H2 generation reactions were observed for all these lower alcohols, and the 
order of reactivity was methanol, ethanol, 1-propanol, and 2-propanol (Fig. 5). The results 
suggest steric hinderance of alcohols may affect the dehydrogenation reaction. In addition, 
this Fe ion catalysis system is indeed applicable in aqueous environments; however, its 
efficiency diminishes considerably under such conditions. A TON of 5.2(SD = 0.16) for 
3 h (TOF of 1.7(SD = 0.054) h–1
) was observed in methanol/water (1/4, mol/mol, 9/16, 
v/v) with FeIIICl3 (0.17 mM) at pH 3.0 under UV light irradiation (250–385 nm, 30 mW). 
Although the TOF value is not high, the production rate of 1.1(SD = 0.034) × 104
 µmol 
gcat–1
 h–1
 is comparable to those reported for representative highly active heterogeneous 
photocatalysts such as Cu–TiO2 and Pt–TiO2 in methanol/water solution, namely 1.8 × 
104
 and 1.2 × 104
 µmol gcat–1
 h–1
, respectively (31, 32).

We further extended this system and applied it to biomass and its derived 
compounds, such as glucose, cellobiose, starch, cellulose, and cedar powder in water. As 
a result, H2 generation was observed from all of these compounds (Table 1). The TON for 
the dehydrogenation of cellulose was determined as 0.024(SD = 0.0019), which 
corresponds to a TOF of 3.3(SD = 0.94) × 10–3
 h–1
. This value is much lower than that of 
homogeneous iridium complex (TON of 60 for 24, TOF of 2.5) (36). Converting the TOF 
for H2 production from cellulose in our system to the production rate yields a value of 
0.020(SD = 0.0058) µmol gcat–1
 h–1
. This rate is also much lower than those of ca. 4000 
and 1320 µmol gcat–1
 h–1
 reported in previous studies using representative highly active 
heterogeneous photocatalysts, TiO2/NiOx@Cg and Pt–TiO2, respectively (37, 38). 
Interestingly, analysis of the gas phase after the photocatalytic dehydrogenation 
reactions of alcohols in water revealed that hydrocarbons were formed together with H2 
(Figs. S4 and S5). Methane was produced from the photocatalytic reaction of ethanol and 
2-propanol in water, while ethane was generated from photocatalytic reaction of 1-
propanol in water (Fig. S5). These results suggest that the well-known Norrish type I 
reaction proceeds through the interaction between the carbonyl group and UV light (39, 
40) after the dehydrogenation reaction. However, during the photocatalytic reaction of 1-
propanol in water, methane and ethylene were produced alongside ethane. The formation 
mechanism of methane and ethylene from 1-propanol is currently unclear.

In conclusion, we successfully produced H2 from methanol as well as other 
alcohols using a remarkably simple catalyst: a free metal ion, the most fundamental unit 
in metal catalysis. Amidst current trends toward increasingly refined catalyst and reaction 
designs, our demonstration of effective catalysis by such a basic species holds important 
implications for both academic research and industrial application. Notably, it was 
revealed that the catalytic activity for the methanol dehydrogenation is on pair with those 
of previous homogeneous catalysts and heterogeneous photocatalysts. Given the 
accessible nature of this catalyst system, its applicability is expected to be broad, 
extending beyond lower alcohols to include biomass and its derivatives. While the 
reaction we discovered is unprecedented and its mechanism remains to be fully elucidated, 
this very simplicity offers new possibilities for exploring metal ion chemistry.
